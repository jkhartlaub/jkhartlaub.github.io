This year's Global Physics Summit [****American Physical Society****](https://www.linkedin.com/company/aps-physics/) in Downtown Denver proved to be the most welcoming space, filled to the brim with physicists from all over the world exchanging the latest results, many of which related to the Quantum Computing Ecosystem ⚛️  
  
𝘎𝘭𝘰𝘣𝘢𝘭 𝘗𝘩𝘺𝘴𝘪𝘤𝘴 𝘚𝘶𝘮𝘮𝘪𝘵, 𝘗𝘢𝘳𝘵 𝘐 — 𝘚𝘤𝘪𝘦𝘯𝘤𝘦  
  
This conference was a prime opportunity to share the progress [****Andreas Alexander Buchheit****](https://www.linkedin.com/in/andreas-alexander-buchheit/) ([****ETH Zürich****](https://www.linkedin.com/school/eth-zurich/)), collaborators, and I ([****Universität des Saarlandes****](https://www.linkedin.com/school/saarland-university/) and [****Deutsches Zentrum für Luft-und Raumfahrt e.V.****](https://www.linkedin.com/company/dlr/), who funded this travel) have made on developing methods and algorithms for long-range interacting systems in terms of zeta functions with a global audience!  
  
Imagine any very large physical system with lots of structure where the particles interact with each other over long distances. Such long-range interacting systems appear everywhere in nature, where the long-range nature of the system gives rise to phenomena not found in systems where particles only interact with each other over short distances. Calculations are also very difficult, as the enormous number of particles in realistically sized physical systems makes it impossible to just add up all interactions together.  
  
In my talk in the "Precision Many-Body Physics I" session last Thursday, I explored how many desirable quantities in such systems can be expressed in terms of the 100-year-old Epstein zeta function. This wide generalization of the famous Riemann zeta function is efficiently implemented by Andreas Alexander Buchheit and our team in Saarbrücken ([****Ruben Gutendorf****](https://www.linkedin.com/in/ruben-gutendorf-9a35a8324/) and [****Jan Schmitz****](https://www.linkedin.com/in/danjujan/)) in [1], with analytic properties described in [2]. This method generalizes to many-body systems [3], with applications in the investigation of the stability of matter in theoretical chemistry, which we explore with collaborators in New Zealand: [****Peter Schwerdtfeger****](https://www.linkedin.com/in/peter-schwerdtfeger-8b90ba26/), [****Andrés Robles Navarro****](https://www.linkedin.com/in/andr%C3%A9s-robles-navarro-941441162/), et al. [4]. In my favorite part of the talk, I presented our very recent stable implementation of high-order derivatives of the Epstein zeta function in a development branch of [1], which enables the efficient calculation of a broad class of anisotropic singular lattice sums. For example, such sums arise in the description of topological superconductors, which we investigate in future work at the German Aerospace Center (together with [****Benedikt Fauseweh****](https://www.linkedin.com/in/benedikt-fauseweh-a872b9244/) and [****Gary Schmiedinghoff****](https://www.linkedin.com/in/gary-schmiedinghoff-282398194/)). Finally, I offered a glimpse of our collaboration with [****Torsten Keßler****](https://www.linkedin.com/in/torsten-kessler/) ([****Eindhoven University of Technology****](https://www.linkedin.com/school/eindhoven-university-of-technology/)) and Filipp Rybakov ([****Uppsala University****](https://www.linkedin.com/school/uppsala-university/)) [5], where we apply those derivatives of Epstein zeta functions in micromagnetics.  
  
The overall goal was to convince the audience, that zeta-related methods can strengthen their research wherever it connects to long-range interacting systems 🌌
