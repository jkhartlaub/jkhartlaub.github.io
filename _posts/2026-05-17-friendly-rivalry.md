There is a (friendly? 😄) rivalry between quantum computational methods and their classical counterparts.  
  
It goes like this: a research group or a (sometimes commercially motivated 💰) company reports a so-called "quantum computing advantage" on a particular problem: Here is a very specific task that takes extremely long or is outright 𝗶𝗻𝘁𝗿𝗮𝗰𝘁𝗮𝗯𝗹𝗲 𝗼𝗻 𝗮 𝗰𝗹𝗮𝘀𝘀𝗶𝗰𝗮𝗹 𝗰𝗼𝗺𝗽𝘂𝘁𝗲𝗿, yet can be performed with 𝗵𝗶𝗴𝗵 𝗮𝗰𝗰𝘂𝗿𝗮𝗰𝘆 𝗼𝗻 𝗮 𝗾𝘂𝗮𝗻𝘁𝘂𝗺 𝗰𝗼𝗺𝗽𝘂𝘁𝗲𝗿 in a 𝗿𝗲𝗮𝘀𝗼𝗻𝗮𝗯𝗹𝗲 𝘁𝗶𝗺𝗲𝗳𝗿𝗮𝗺𝗲.  
  
This then sparks a competition among researchers to 𝗿𝗲𝗽𝗹𝗶𝗰𝗮𝘁𝗲 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝗿𝗲𝘀𝘂𝗹𝘁 on a purely 𝗰𝗹𝗮𝘀𝘀𝗶𝗰𝗮𝗹 𝗺𝗮𝗰𝗵𝗶𝗻𝗲, preferably one with horrible specs to add insult to injury.  
  
Many of those feats are based on 𝘁𝗲𝗻𝘀𝗼𝗿 𝗺𝗲𝘁𝗵𝗼𝗱𝘀! My colleague [****Kevin Lively****](https://www.linkedin.com/in/kevin-lively-372001112/) ([****German Aerospace Center (DLR)****](https://www.linkedin.com/company/dlr/)) argues that we can understand this opposition between the classical and quantum approaches as a fruitful synergy ⚛️ a point he made at the Mini-Workshop on Quantum Technologies hosted by [****RWTH Aachen University****](https://www.linkedin.com/school/rwth-aachen-university/) last week.  
  
Our host [****Markus Bachmayr****](https://www.linkedin.com/in/markus-bachmayr-94a27737/) employed tensor networks to tame the high-dimensional nature of the electronic Schrödinger equation in 𝗾𝘂𝗮𝗻𝘁𝘂𝗺 𝗰𝗵𝗲𝗺𝗶𝘀𝘁𝗿𝘆 (and also invited us to a sublime noodle restaurant in the evening 🍜)  
  
The core challenge is already clear: 𝗿𝗲𝗱𝘂𝗰𝗲 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝗰𝗼𝗺𝗽𝗹𝗲𝘅𝗶𝘁𝘆 of a high-dimensional problem while 𝗿𝗲𝘁𝗮𝗶𝗻𝗶𝗻𝗴 𝗮𝗰𝗰𝘂𝗿𝗮𝗰𝘆. Tensor networks offer a promising route. Start small, then scale up to high dimensions 📈 In contrast, [****Andreas Alexander Buchheit****](https://www.linkedin.com/in/andreas-alexander-buchheit/) and I employ so-called 𝗺𝗮𝗻𝘆-𝗯𝗼𝗱𝘆 𝘇𝗲𝘁𝗮 𝗳𝘂𝗻𝗰𝘁𝗶𝗼𝗻𝘀 to tackle computations in high-dimensional problems in theoretical chemistry [1, 2] 🔢  
  
The solution is unclear, though I think a combination of all of the best methods and approaches will prevail ⚛️
