There is a (friendly? 😄) rivalry between quantum computational methods and their classical counterparts.

It goes like this: a research group or a (sometimes commercially motivated 💰) company reports a so-called "quantum computing advantage" on a particular problem: Here is a very specific task that takes extremely long or is outright **intractable on a classical computer**, yet can be performed with **high accuracy on a quantum computer** in a **reasonable timeframe**.

This then sparks a competition among researchers to **replicate the result** on a purely **classical machine**, preferably one with horrible specs to add insult to injury.

Many of those feats are based on **tensor methods**! My colleague [Kevin Lively](https://www.linkedin.com/in/kevin-lively-372001112/) ([German Aerospace Center (DLR)](https://www.linkedin.com/company/dlr/)) argues that we can understand this opposition between the classical and quantum approaches as a fruitful synergy ⚛️ a point he made at the Mini-Workshop on Quantum Technologies hosted by [RWTH Aachen University](https://www.linkedin.com/school/rwth-aachen-university/) last week.

Our host [Markus Bachmayr](https://www.linkedin.com/in/markus-bachmayr-94a27737/) employed tensor networks to tame the high-dimensional nature of the electronic Schrödinger equation in **quantum chemistry** (and also invited us to a sublime noodle restaurant in the evening 🍜)

The core challenge is already clear: **reduce the complexity** of a high-dimensional problem while **retaining accuracy**. Tensor networks offer a promising route. Start small, then scale up to high dimensions 📈 In contrast, [Andreas Alexander Buchheit](https://www.linkedin.com/in/andreas-alexander-buchheit/) and I employ so-called **many-body zeta functions** to tackle computations in high-dimensional problems in theoretical chemistry [[1](#ref-1), [2](#ref-2)] 🔢

The solution is unclear, though I think a combination of all of the best methods and approaches will prevail ⚛️
